\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Evidence base.}
This synthesis uses both project PRISMA runs: 6{,}663 raw paper records before
cross-run deduplication, plus 119 stored full-text review artifacts.  We use
the broad corpus to identify recurring problems, then cite representative
primary papers for each problem.  A citation below is evidence for that
paper's method or evaluation target, not evidence that our proposed policy
already works.

\paragraph{One user is the unit of personalization.}
For user $u$, the offline history
\[
  \history
  =
  \{\text{thoughts, conversations, corrections, preferences,
  task states, actions, tools, and outcomes}\}
\]
produces an initial checkpoint $c_u^{(0)}$.  After deployment, interaction
batch $\stream$ is generated by the active checkpoint $\checkpoint$.
An update algorithm $\mathcal A$ emits
\begin{equation}
  c_u^{(t+1)}
  =
  \mathcal A\!\left(c_u^{(t)},\history,D_u^{(1:t)}\right).
  \label{eq:update}
\end{equation}
Every record names its user, time, task, generating checkpoint, action trace,
feedback source, and training eligibility.  A shared backbone or population
prior is allowed, but another user's raw history cannot silently enter user
$u$'s deployed branch.

\paragraph{A policy update is required.}
Retrieval, profiles, memory, and decoding control are strong baselines, not
the learned object.  OPPU stores one user's history in a private PEFT module
\citep{tan2024oppu}; P-RLHF learns user-conditioned policy parameters from
preference pairs \citep{li2024personalized}; and SPRInG updates one user
adapter over chronological periods \citep{kim2026spring}.  These are the
closest parameter-learning precedents.  CoSteer instead performs tuning-free
local delta steering at decoding time \citep{lv2025costeer}, while FABLE
updates a small external execution policy around a frozen host agent
\citep{jin2026fable}.  Both are important deployment baselines, but neither is
the same claim as improving the user's language-model policy weights.

\paragraph{The objective is constrained, not a single blended score.}
The method should improve future personalization while preserving task
competence and safety:
\begin{align}
  \max_{\mathcal A}\quad&
  \mathbb E[\upers(c_u^{(t+1)})] \nonumber\\
  \text{s.t.}\quad&
  \mathbb E[\utask(c_u^{(t+1)})-\utask(c_u^{(t)})]
    \ge -\delta_{\mathrm{task}}, \nonumber\\
  &
  \mathbb E[\usafe(c_u^{(t+1)})-\usafe(c_u^{(t)})]
    \ge -\delta_{\mathrm{safe}}, \nonumber\\
  &
  \cost_{\mathrm{train}},\cost_{\mathrm{serve}},
  \cost_{\mathrm{feedback}}
  \ \text{remain within declared budgets}.
  \label{eq:constrained-objective}
\end{align}
Task success and personalization are therefore co-primary measurements.  One
cannot compensate numerically for the failure of the other.

\section{Exact-Match Filter}
\label{sec:filter}

A paper belongs to our exact setting only when it satisfies all five
conditions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{A\arabic*.}]
  \item it maintains a persistent policy for one identified user;
  \item it initializes that policy from the same user's historical evidence;
  \item after deployment, new evidence from that user causes further updates;
  \item the policy acts in an agent task with actions, tools, or execution
  decisions; and
  \item the updated object includes parameters of the language-model policy,
  rather than only a profile, memory, reward model, decoder, or external
  controller.
\end{enumerate}
Here \emph{online} has a strict meaning: same-user evidence arrives after
deployment and is followed by a parameter update.  Fresh rollouts collected
inside one centralized training job are useful online-training evidence, but
they are not by themselves post-deployment continual personalization.

No cited method satisfies all five conditions.  The problems below are
therefore ranked by how directly they define the missing method, rather than
by how often related keywords appear in prior work.

\section{Problems Studied in This Setting}
\label{sec:problems}

\subsection{Learning the user}

\paragraph{P1: identify preferences from sparse, mixed evidence.}
Aggregating feedback across users can erase minority or conflicting
preferences \citep{li2024personalized,jang2023personalizedsoups}.  A single
user also supplies heterogeneous evidence: explicit requests, implicit
choices, edits, accepted outputs, and task outcomes do not share one label
space.  CoPL and PROPER borrow structure from related users to mitigate sparse
histories \citep{choi2025copl,zhang2025proper}; FSPO studies rapid adaptation
from few preference examples \citep{singh2025fspo}.  The open question for us
is whether one policy can use the target user's complete history without
reducing it to a textual profile.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 2, necessary support.
\textbf{One user:} mixed; the methods represent individual preferences, but
several learn through shared users, groups, or population structure.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} yes.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} no.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} no; the evidence concerns response preference or
alignment.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} mixed; P-RLHF, PROPER, and FSPO train
generative-policy parameters, whereas CoPL primarily trains a personalized
reward model.
\textbf{Difference from us:} these papers infer preferences from fixed
datasets; they do not update one deployed user's agent policy from later task
trajectories.
\end{settingpointer}

\paragraph{P2: separate persistent preference from transient context.}
A recent request may reflect a lasting preference, a temporary task
constraint, or noise.  SPRInG treats this as a drift-selection problem before
updating a user adapter \citep{kim2026spring}.  In an agent, the distinction is
harder because failure may come from task reasoning, tool execution, or a
wrong preference assumption.  A useful update must identify which part of the
trace is evidence about the user.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 2, necessary support.
\textbf{One user:} yes; SPRInG maintains a distinct adapter and memory for
each user.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} yes, from period-0 user history.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} no; later periods are a retrospective
chronological simulation over a fixed corpus.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} no; evaluation is personalized text generation.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} yes, through per-user LoRA updates.
\textbf{Difference from us:} SPRInG supplies a close continual-personalization
component, but not live agent trajectories or a deployed offline-to-online
agent loop.
\end{settingpointer}

\paragraph{P3: handle cold start without losing ownership.}
Sharing population structure improves few-shot adaptation, as explored by
collaborative, group-level, and federated personalized models
\citep{choi2025copl,zhang2025proper,zhu2026fedpdpo}.  Yet unrestricted
cross-user transfer conflicts with the one-user ownership boundary.  The
problem is to use a consented prior while keeping the target user's updates,
state, and evaluation distinct.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 4, optional scope.
\textbf{One user:} partial; a target user or client is represented, but the
methods deliberately borrow structure across users.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} yes.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} no.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} no.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} mixed; PROPER and FedPDPO train
personalized language-model components, while CoPL's personalized object is a
reward model.
\textbf{Difference from us:} these are collaborative or federated offline
personalization methods, whereas our current claim starts with a known user's
own history and does not require population-assisted cold start.
\end{settingpointer}

\subsection{Moving from offline history to online learning}

\paragraph{P4: build an offline initializer with online headroom.}
The offline checkpoint must already complete tasks, otherwise online
interaction produces little useful feedback.  It must also remain plastic.
OPPU supplies a practical per-user initializer \citep{tan2024oppu}; WebRL
shows why a warm start matters for sparse-reward agent learning
\citep{qi2025webrl}.  High offline fit alone is not enough if the resulting
policy cannot improve from new evidence.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 1, method-defining.
\textbf{One user:} split; OPPU is per-user, while WebRL has no user identity.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} yes in both lines.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} no; WebRL's fresh rollouts occur
inside benchmark training, and OPPU is offline-only.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} split; WebRL is an agent but not personalized,
whereas OPPU is personalized but not an agent method.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} yes.
\textbf{Difference from us:} the two papers supply complementary halves, but
neither initializes and then continually updates one user's deployed agent
policy.
\end{settingpointer}

\paragraph{P5: cross the offline--online boundary without a performance dip.}
Offline records were produced by older policies and may not cover actions
preferred by the current policy.  The LLM literature studies policy-generator
synchronization \citep{lanchantin2025bridging}; hybrid preference learning
uses offline pairs to reduce online queries \citep{bose2024hybrid}; and policy
expansion protects useful offline behavior during exploration
\citep{zhang2023pex}.  Our version must additionally preserve user identity,
feedback provenance, and checkpoint lineage.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 1, method-defining.
\textbf{One user:} no.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} yes.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} no in our strict sense; ``online''
denotes fresh training-time rollouts or queries.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} no.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} mixed; Bridging updates an LLM policy,
while hybrid preference optimization and policy expansion are broader
optimization formulations.
\textbf{Difference from us:} these methods solve the optimization handoff
without same-user deployment, personal checkpoint lineage, or personalized
agent evidence.
\end{settingpointer}

\paragraph{P6: adapt to drift without forgetting or chasing noise.}
Continual personalization must learn changed preferences while retaining
stable ones.  SPRInG selects high-drift interactions and keeps residual
memory \citep{kim2026spring}.  Asynchronous LLM training adds another source
of staleness because behavior and learner policies diverge
\citep{huang2026stable}.  Success therefore requires both preference-level
retention and valid use of old trajectories.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 1, method-defining.
\textbf{One user:} split; SPRInG is per-user, while Stable Asynchrony is not
personalized.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} yes for SPRInG; Stable Asynchrony has no
personalized offline initializer.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} no; both evaluations remain
training-time or retrospective.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} no.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} yes.
\textbf{Difference from us:} SPRInG addresses preference drift and Stable
Asynchrony addresses stale LLM-RL trajectories, but neither combines both in
a deployed personalized agent.
\end{settingpointer}

\paragraph{P7: learn quickly while minimizing user burden.}
Online feedback is expensive.  The method should choose interactions that
distinguish plausible user preferences, rather than requesting labels for
easy or redundant examples.  Hybrid preference optimization formalizes the
offline-data versus online-query tradeoff \citep{bose2024hybrid}; FABLE is a
pure-online method that learns a per-user execution policy as a contextual
bandit \citep{jin2026fable}.  In our setting, quick adaptation means fewer
real interactions or corrections to reach a fixed future-task utility, not
merely fewer optimizer steps.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 3, secondary claim.
\textbf{One user:} mixed; FABLE is per-user, while hybrid preference
optimization is not.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} split; hybrid optimization uses offline
preferences, whereas FABLE has no learned offline initializer.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} yes for FABLE, which is pure online;
not in the target sense for the hybrid method.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} yes for FABLE.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} no for FABLE; it updates a small
external execution policy around a frozen host agent.
\textbf{Difference from us:} FABLE is the closest pure-online personalized
agent baseline, but our target joins a historical LLM-policy initializer to
later updates of that LLM policy.
\end{settingpointer}

\subsection{Learning an agent policy}

\paragraph{P8: preserve task completion while personalizing execution.}
Personalized agents must choose tools, memory, clarifications, action order,
and response style while still finishing the task.  PersonalWAB evaluates
personalized web actions \citep{cai2025personalwab}; FingerTip 20K separates
proactive suggestions from personalized mobile execution
\citep{yang2026fingertip}; and pure-online FABLE separates an adaptive
execution policy from frozen host-agent reasoning \citep{jin2026fable}.  These
works expose why response preference alone is insufficient: the same final
answer may hide a bad or unwanted trajectory.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 1, method-defining.
\textbf{One user:} partial; all model personal behavior, but only FABLE
maintains a persistent online execution policy for the individual user.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} present only in the offline
training/evaluation line, not in FABLE.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} yes only for FABLE.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} yes.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} not in the exact combination;
PersonalWAB and FingerTip do not establish persistent post-deployment
per-user updates, and FABLE updates a non-LLM controller.
\textbf{Difference from us:} these works establish the agent setting and its
evaluation, but not one LLM policy that crosses from personal history into
continued same-user parameter training.
\end{settingpointer}

\paragraph{P9: assign credit from long, partially observed trajectories.}
The user may provide one correction after many reasoning and tool steps.
Sparse task outcomes say whether the task ended well, but not which step
expressed the user's preference.  WebRL addresses sparse terminal feedback
and policy drift for web agents \citep{qi2025webrl}; our problem adds
user-conditioned credit and delayed preference evidence.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 1, method-defining.
\textbf{One user:} no.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} yes.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} no; WebRL collects rollouts during a
centralized training procedure.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} no; it trains an LLM web agent without persistent
user identity.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} yes.
\textbf{Difference from us:} WebRL supplies sparse-reward agent-learning
machinery, but its credit is task-conditioned rather than user-conditioned
and does not come from a deployed personal stream.
\end{settingpointer}

\subsection{Obtaining credible and affordable evidence}

\paragraph{P10: use simulators without training to simulator artifacts.}
UserRL, UserVille, ProPerSim, and CollabLLM use simulated interaction to train
or evaluate user-facing agents
\citep{qian2025userrl,sun2025userville,kim2025propersim,wu2025collabllm}.
PersonalLLM also creates heterogeneous synthetic preference models
\citep{zollo2024personalllm}.  Simulation makes online learning affordable,
but it can encode the simulator's style and reward biases.  Reward-model
accuracy itself may fail to predict downstream personalization quality
\citep{rezk2025rmcrisis}.  A simulator can generate training experience; it
cannot be the sole judge of a real-user claim.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 4, optional scope.
\textbf{One user:} no persistent target-user policy; simulators instantiate
many users or personas for a shared system.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} mixed and method-dependent.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} no.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} partial; several papers train or evaluate
user-facing agents, but under simulated users.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} mixed; these are primarily simulator,
data, or training infrastructures rather than one common update algorithm.
\textbf{Difference from us:} simulation can supply experience for our method,
but cannot establish that one real user's deployed LLM policy improves.
\end{settingpointer}

\paragraph{P11: make per-user deployment cheaper than repeated large-model use.}
Dedicated adapters create storage and serving overhead, while full online
training adds accelerator cost.  OPPU motivates lightweight user ownership
\citep{tan2024oppu}; CoSteer keeps context local while steering a cloud model
\citep{lv2025costeer}; and pure-online FABLE moves adaptation into a small
external execution policy \citep{jin2026fable}.  Our stronger efficiency
question is whether a 4B, 7B, or 32B user policy can match or exceed a much
larger generic model on user-conditioned utility at lower serving cost.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 3, secondary claim.
\textbf{One user:} yes; all three methods expose a user-specific component or
context.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} yes only for OPPU's learned per-user adapter.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} yes only for pure-online FABLE.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} yes only for FABLE.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} yes only for offline OPPU; CoSteer is
tuning-free decoding-time local delta steering, and FABLE trains an external
execution policy rather than the host LLM.
\textbf{Difference from us:} each method satisfies a different efficiency
slice, but none trains a compact personal LLM first offline and then from its
deployed agent experience.
\end{settingpointer}

\paragraph{P12: evaluate future behavior rather than history recall.}
LongLaMP provides user-disjoint and chronological long-form tasks
\citep{kumar2024longlamp}.  PrefEval tests explicit and implicit preference
following across long conversations \citep{zhao2025prefeval}.  PERMA adds
gradual preference formation, temporal interference, and task completion
\citep{liu2026perma}; PRISM supplies individualized human feedback
\citep{kirk2024prism}.  These benchmarks show that retrieval accuracy is not
the endpoint.  The endpoint is correct behavior on an untouched future task.

\begin{settingpointer}
\textbf{Role:} Tier 2, necessary support.
\textbf{One user:} yes at the data or evaluation level.
\textbf{Offline initialization:} not applicable; these are datasets and
benchmarks.
\textbf{Post-deployment online update:} no.
\textbf{Personalized agent:} mixed; PERMA has an agent-oriented environment,
while LongLaMP, PrefEval, and PRISM primarily supply text or preference
evidence.
\textbf{LLM-policy parameter training:} no method is supplied.
\textbf{Difference from us:} these resources can test our policy, but they do
not define the offline-to-online update algorithm.
\end{settingpointer}

\section{The Claim We Should Make}
\label{sec:claim}

\subsection{Central claim}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{A compact, user-owned language-model policy can be initialized from
one person's heterogeneous historical interactions and then improved from
that person's later agent-task evidence through real parameter updates, while
preserving task success and safety.}
\end{quote}

This claim joins three pieces that prior work usually separates:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Personal policy initialization.}  The offline stage learns a
  persistent user-conditioned policy, rather than only retrieving a profile.
  \item \textbf{Real offline-to-online adaptation.}  Later evidence causes
  positive parameter updates and new loadable checkpoints.
  \item \textbf{Agent validity.}  Improvement is measured on both
  personalized behavior and task completion over untouched future tasks.
\end{enumerate}

The contribution is therefore the \emph{phase-coupling algorithm}: how
historical evidence, fresh trajectories, feedback, replay, and checkpoint
activation interact for one user.  The contribution is not the existence of
LoRA, retrieval, a user simulator, or an online optimizer by itself.

\subsection{Secondary claims}

These claims should appear only when their dedicated tests pass:
\begin{description}
  \item[Fast adaptation.] The method reaches a fixed utility with fewer real
  interactions than SPRInG-style selective SFT, naive cumulative fine-tuning,
  and online-only learning.
  \item[Trajectory advantage.] Tool actions and verified outcomes improve
  future personalized execution beyond text-only user history.
  \item[Small versus large.] A compact personalized policy beats a larger
  generic model on personalization while remaining task-non-inferior and
  cheaper to serve.
  \item[Simulation benefit.] Simulator experience lowers real-user sample
  requirements without changing the ordering of methods under held-out human
  evaluation.
  \item[Privacy by design.] The implementation satisfies a declared data-flow
  boundary and does not expose another user's raw records.  Local PEFT alone
  is not evidence for this claim.
\end{description}

\section{Success Criteria}
\label{sec:success}

\subsection{Hard validity gates}

Before comparing scores, every reported update must pass:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the parent and candidate checkpoint hashes differ;
  \item the candidate loads in a fresh worker and actually generates the
  evaluated trajectories;
  \item offline, online-adaptation, validation, and future-test intervals are
  chronological and disjoint;
  \item every training record has user, task, behavior-policy, checkpoint,
  timestamp, and feedback provenance;
  \item failed runs, users, and rollbacks remain in the report;
  \item the simulator, reward model, or LLM judge has not seen the future-test
  target or its answer.
\end{enumerate}
Failure of any item invalidates the run rather than lowering its score.

\subsection{Primary statistical gate}

Let $b$ be the strongest eligible baseline on the validation set.  For each
user-period cluster, evaluate the candidate and $b$ on identical future
tasks.  Define
\begin{align}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{pers}}
    &= \upers(c_{\mathrm{candidate}})-\upers(c_b),\\
  \Delta_{\mathrm{task}}
    &= \utask(c_{\mathrm{candidate}})-\utask(c_b).
\end{align}
The main claim passes only when
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{LCB}_{95\%}(\Delta_{\mathrm{pers}})>0
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \operatorname{LCB}_{95\%}(\Delta_{\mathrm{task}})
  \ge -\delta_{\mathrm{task}}.
  \label{eq:primary-gate}
\end{equation}
The minimum meaningful personalization effect and
$\delta_{\mathrm{task}}$ must be fixed before final evaluation.  Confidence
intervals are clustered by user or task lineage, not by treating correlated
turns as independent samples.

\subsection{What ``beat a large model'' means}

The large-model comparison must include:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a large generic model without personal history;
  \item the same model with the full allowed history;
  \item strong RAG or profile prompting;
  \item a decoding-time method such as CoSteer when executable
  \citep{lv2025costeer};
  \item the compact offline-only and compact online-adapted policies.
\end{enumerate}
Prompts, tools, history access, and task budgets must match.  We may claim that
the compact model beats the large model only if it improves personalization,
meets task non-inferiority, and costs less.  A win on preference style alone
does not establish superior task capability.

\subsection{What quick adaptation means}

Report utility after every fixed number of real interactions, not merely after
epochs.  For threshold $\tau$,
\begin{equation}
  T_{\tau}
  =
  \min\{n:\upers(c_u^{(n)})\ge\tau
  \ \text{and task/safety gates pass}\}.
\end{equation}
Also report area under the learning curve, final utility, number of
clarifications, user correction time, and rollbacks.  A method is faster only
if it reaches the same valid target with less user evidence or wall-clock
cost.

\subsection{What simulator success means}

Simulation has three separate tests:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Preference fidelity:} agreement and calibration on held-out
  real-user comparisons.
  \item \textbf{Method fidelity:} rank correlation between methods under the
  simulator and under real-user evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Transfer value:} fewer real interactions are needed after
  simulator pretraining than without it.
\end{enumerate}
Passing only simulator reward permits a simulator result, not a real-user
personalization claim.

\paragraph{Evaluation substrates.}
Use at least one chronological generation stream such as LongLaMP
\citep{kumar2024longlamp} and one executable agent substrate such as
PersonalWAB or FingerTip 20K
\citep{cai2025personalwab,yang2026fingertip}.  PrefEval and PERMA provide
additional long-horizon preference tests
\citep{zhao2025prefeval,liu2026perma}.  User simulators can support training,
but the final claim needs held-out human feedback or frozen real-user
records.  A result on one substrate is a feasibility result; a general claim
requires at least two materially different substrates.

\paragraph{Replication.}
Although each deployed policy belongs to one user, a paper should repeat the
same protocol over many independent users or user streams.  Report per-user
results, the median, the worst decile, and cluster-aware intervals.  Use at
least three seeds for feasibility and five for final claims.

\section{Claims We Must Not Make}
\label{sec:nonclaims}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Chronological offline replay is not post-deployment online learning.
  \item Better retrieval or profile recall is not evidence of a better policy.
  \item A simulator-only win is not evidence of real-user improvement.
  \item A changed loss value is not evidence that the candidate checkpoint was
  loaded.
  \item A personalized reward-model gain is not automatically a policy or task
  gain \citep{rezk2025rmcrisis}.
  \item Local adapters do not by themselves prove privacy.
  \item Beating a large model on style does not mean beating it on task
  capability.
  \item Mean improvement cannot hide severe failures for a subset of users.
\end{itemize}

\section{Recommended Paper Position}

The strongest paper is not a survey of every personalization problem.  It is
an algorithm paper about the transition from \emph{one user's historical
evidence} to \emph{one user's continually improving policy}.  Data adaptation
defines the offline input.  Offline-to-online optimization is the method.
Task-preserving future personalization is the primary result.  Quick
adaptation, simulator-assisted training, privacy, and small-model efficiency
are valuable secondary claims, each earned by its own test.
